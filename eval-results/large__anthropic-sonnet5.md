## Narrative Summary

Across the three solicitations, the Pacific Northwest/Intermountain region is seeing simultaneous, IRP-driven procurement for capacity and energy resources over a similar timeframe (roughly 2026–2032), with Avista and Idaho Power specifying the largest quantified needs — up to ~425 MW of summer capacity and ~415 MW of winter capacity for Avista, and at least 200 MW of incremental peak capacity for Idaho Power concentrated in 2031–2032. PSE's solicitation, while framed around CETA compliance and utility-scale clean resources, does not specify a quantified MW/GWh target in the retrieved excerpts, but its eligible resource list (wind, solar, storage, gas with fuel-supply requirements, biomass) substantially overlaps with the resource types sought by Avista and Idaho Power. This overlap means all three utilities are likely competing for a similar developer and resource pool — wind, solar, storage, and gas-fired/tolling assets — within the same regional interconnection queues and construction supply chains, which could compress developer bandwidth and elevate pricing/lead-time risk for bidders participating in multiple RFPs concurrently.

Interconnection and deliverability requirements also point to potential clustering bottlenecks. Avista's RFP is explicitly tied to its 2025 Cluster Study, and its own posted transmission-capacity table shows very limited availability at key delivery points (0 MW available at WA Mid-C, only 8 MW at Eastern Montana), meaning bidders proposing off-system or Mid-C-delivered resources may face binding transmission constraints. Idaho Power similarly requires bidders to demonstrate an active GI queue position and fund both ICIF and TPIF interconnection facilities, reinforcing that resources without a mature interconnection footprint in the IPC BA will struggle to compete. PSE's geographic restriction — POI must sit on PSE's own transmission system west of the Cascades, or else secure transmission to a specified POD — similarly narrows the practical resource pool to projects with either existing PSE-territory interconnection or confirmed deliverability arrangements.

Taken together, these three solicitations suggest that resources sited within or near each utility's own balancing authority area (or with pre-existing, advanced-stage interconnection queue positions) will have a meaningfully better competitive position than off-system resources reliant on scarce regional transmission capacity. Given the overlapping timing (mid-2020s through early 2030s) and shared reliance on WECC/WRAP-area transmission paths, bidders with projects that could serve multiple LSEs (e.g., wind or storage sited near shared transmission corridors like BPA or Mid-C) may find themselves choosing between solicitations rather than serving all three, further tightening the effective regional supply of deliverable capacity.
